How to Start Real Estate Investing in Canada With No Experience: Three Ways to Buy

Three ways to buy, what lenders and the CRA say, and the checks to make before you buy.

When I wanted to invest in real estate, I had no one to ask. No parent, sibling or relative who had done it. I noticed that many wealthy people hold real estate, so I believed it could be a good investment. What I did not know was how to start, and I did not want to start blindly. This guide shares what I learned, and the simple framework I teach my students: there are three kinds of buyers. It is education, not advice, and real estate carries risk.

My story, in short

I invested in a mentor. I joined a one-year mentorship program and paid about 3,000 USD to learn from people who had done it. I learned how to look for a first property that could produce income, and I took notes as I went. When I was ready to buy, I was strategic. I looked for a property that could help pay for itself. I used part of it as a short-term rental, and I lived in the basement. A year later I bought a second property, and a year after that a third. I am now working on a fourth.

This is my own experience. It is not typical, it is not a promise, and I made mistakes along the way. Real estate can also lose money.

The three kinds of buyers

I tell my students that most people who enter real estate fall into one of three groups:

  1. The live-in buyer. You buy a home mainly to live in it.
  2. The cash-flow buyer. You buy a property you do not live in, and it is rented out to produce income.
  3. The both buyer. You live in part of the property and rent out the rest. A duplex, a home with a basement unit, or a small multi-unit building are common examples. Some people call this house hacking.

Each group needs a different plan, different financing and different questions. Choosing the right group first saves a lot of mistakes.

What lenders and the CRA say, in plain words

  • Financing. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada explains how much you need for a down payment, how to choose a mortgage and how pre-approval works. Minimums and rules change, so always check the current figures.
  • Rental income and lenders. CMHC explains how lenders may count rental income when you qualify. The rules differ depending on the number of units and whether you live in the property.
  • Newcomers. The Government of Canada has a page for newcomers buying a home in Canada.
  • Tax. Rental income must be reported. The CRA explains how in completing Form T776. If you rent only part of your home, only the matching share of costs may be deducted.
  • Your credit. Lenders look closely at your credit history. See how to build credit in Canada as a newcomer.

Check these before you buy anything

  • Local rules. Zoning, secondary-suite rules and short-term rental bylaws vary by city. Check yours first.
  • Your lender's terms. Some mortgages restrict how you may use the property.
  • The numbers. Know the full costs, not only the mortgage payment: taxes, insurance, repairs, vacancy and reserves.
  • Your own situation. Your income, credit and goals decide which kind of buyer you should be.

What most beginners get wrong

  • Buying blindly because a property looks like a good deal.
  • Ignoring local rental rules until after they buy.
  • Copying a strategy from another country or city without checking the rules.
  • Learning alone, with no mentor and no one to ask.

Frequently asked questions

Can I start real estate investing with no experience?

Yes, many people do, but learn first. Study the rules, the numbers and your options before you buy, and consider a mentor.

What is house hacking?

Living in part of a property and renting out the rest, so that rent helps cover the cost. Rules and financing differ by city and lender.

Do I need a lot of money to start?

You need a down payment and enough savings for costs and reserves. The FCAC explains current down payment rules.

Is short-term rental allowed everywhere?

No. Many cities restrict it, and some mortgages and leases do too. Check your city and your lender first.

Do I pay tax on rental income?

Yes. Rental income must be reported to the CRA, using the form the CRA describes. Ask an accountant about your situation.

Is real estate a safe investment?

No investment is risk free. Property values and rents can fall, and costs can rise. Never invest money you cannot afford to lose.
