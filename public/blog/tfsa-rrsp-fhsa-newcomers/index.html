TFSA vs RRSP vs FHSA: A Plain Guide for Newcomers to Canada

How the three accounts compare, how room works for newcomers, and building a credit history.

Canada has three common registered accounts for saving: the Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA), the Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) and the First Home Savings Account (FHSA). They work differently, and the rules for newcomers depend on when you became a Canadian resident for tax purposes. This guide explains the differences using the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) pages. It is general education, not personal financial advice.

A "registered account" is a type of account, not an investment. You choose what to hold inside it (cash, GICs, funds or shares) with your bank or investment provider.

TFSA

The CRA's Tax-free Savings Account page explains how a TFSA works, who can open one and how to check your room. Key points:

  • The 2026 annual limit is $7,000.
  • Newcomers start with no past room. Contribution room builds from the year you become a Canadian resident (and are 18 or older), not from the year you turn 18 abroad. Check your room in your CRA My Account, because the amount depends on your residency dates.
  • Withdrawals are tax-free and the amount can be recontributed in a later year.

RRSP

The CRA explains that your RRSP deduction limit is the lower of 18% of your previous year's earned income and the annual dollar limit. The CRA lists the limit for 2026 as $33,810 (see How contributions affect your RRSP deduction limit).

  • Room comes from Canadian earnings. It is based on income you report on a Canadian tax return, so file a return each year. Your personal limit appears on your notice of assessment.
  • Contributions reduce your taxable income, and withdrawals are taxed as income.
  • Contributing more than your limit (beyond a small cushion) can trigger a monthly tax. See the CRA page above.

FHSA

The FHSA page from the CRA explains that:

  • You get $8,000 of participation room in the year you open your first FHSA, and another $8,000 each year after, with a lifetime limit of $40,000. The CRA describes how unused room can carry forward, so read the current rules.
  • Room only starts when you open the account.
  • You must meet the first-time-buyer rules and be a Canadian resident. Read the definitions on the CRA page before you open one.
  • The CRA says you must also file Schedule 15 with your return for the year you open your first FHSA, even if you made no contributions.

Which one first?

There is no single answer, and it depends on your income, tax rate, goals and timeline. Common questions to ask yourself:

  • Saving for a first home within a few years? The FHSA is designed for that.
  • Want flexibility and may need the money? A TFSA lets you withdraw tax-free.
  • Earning a higher income and saving for retirement? An RRSP's deduction may matter more.

A fee-only financial planner, or the free tools from the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC), can help you decide for your situation.

Building a credit history as a newcomer

Canadian credit bureaus record your activity in Canada, so credit history from another country generally does not appear on your Canadian credit report. Canada.ca suggests newcomers without a credit history consider a secured credit card, where a deposit acts as your limit. Paying bills on time and in full is the habit that matters most. Read the FCAC's credit report and score basics and how to improve your credit score. For a fuller plan, read how to build credit in Canada as a newcomer.

Protect yourself

Be wary of anyone who promises guaranteed investment returns, asks you to transfer money to "unlock" an account, or pressures you to decide fast. For online-income ideas, read side hustles in Canada and real estate investing in Canada: the basics. No guide can promise results, and results vary.

Frequently asked questions

Can newcomers open a TFSA?

Generally yes, if you are a Canadian resident for tax purposes and 18 or older. Check the CRA page for the current eligibility rules.

Do I get TFSA room for the years before I arrived?

No. Room starts building from the year you become a resident. Check your CRA My Account for your exact room.

Do I need Canadian income to have RRSP room?

RRSP room is based on earned income reported on a Canadian tax return. Without Canadian earned income, you may have little or no room.

Can I have an FHSA and a TFSA?

Yes. They are separate accounts with separate limits.

What happens if I put in too much?

Each account has penalties for over-contributing. Check your limit before depositing, using your CRA My Account.

Is a registered account the same as an investment?

No. It is a tax-sheltered container. What you hold inside it carries its own risks.
