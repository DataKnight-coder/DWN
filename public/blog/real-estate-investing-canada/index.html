Real Estate Investing in Canada: A Beginner's Guide to the Basics

Costs, financing, tax and risk explained simply, with official sources and no promised returns.

Property can be part of a long-term plan for building assets, but it is a large, slow and risky commitment, and it is not suited to everyone. This guide explains the basics in plain language: the kinds of costs involved, how financing works at a high level, the main risks, how rental income is taxed and what newcomers should know. It does not recommend any investment, it does not promise returns, and it is not financial, legal or tax advice. Rules, rates and limits change often, so use the official links and speak to qualified professionals before you act.

Start with your goal and your numbers

Before you look at a listing, answer three questions in writing:

  1. Why do I want property? A home to live in, rental income, long-term growth or a mix are different goals with different risks.
  2. What can I afford if things go wrong? Plan for months with no tenant, higher interest rates and unexpected repairs.
  3. What is my time horizon? Property usually takes years, not months, and buying and selling carry large costs.

The real costs of owning property

Many beginners focus on the purchase price and forget the rest. Budget for:

  • Down payment and closing costs, which can include legal fees, inspections and land transfer taxes that differ by province and city.
  • Mortgage payments, which include interest and can change when you renew.
  • Property taxes, insurance and utilities.
  • Maintenance and repairs, which are a constant cost, not an occasional one.
  • Vacancy, the time a rental stands empty.
  • Condo or strata fees, if the property is a condominium.

How financing works, at a high level

Most buyers use a mortgage. Lenders look at your income, debts, credit history and down payment. In Canada, a smaller down payment generally means you must pay for mortgage loan insurance, which adds to your costs. The current rules and limits change, so read CMHC's pages on mortgage loan insurance and speak to a mortgage professional about your situation. Rental properties can be treated differently from homes you live in, so ask lenders specifically about investment financing.

Newcomers: building a Canadian credit history takes time, and lenders may ask for different documents. CMHC publishes a guide, Buying Your First Home in Canada: What Newcomers Need to Know, that explains the process step by step.

Rental income and tax

If you rent out a property, the income is generally taxable. The CRA explains that owners report rental income and expenses on Form T776. See its page on completing Form T776. In general:

  • Reasonable costs of earning the rental income, such as repairs and certain operating costs, can be deducted.
  • Major improvements and the cost of buying the property are generally treated as capital costs, recovered over time rather than deducted at once.
  • Mortgage principal repayments are not deductible.
  • Co-owners report their share according to their ownership.
  • Keep invoices and records, because the CRA may ask for them.

Selling a property can also have tax consequences, depending on whether it was your principal residence. Ask an accountant before you buy or sell.

Landlord and tenant rules

Residential tenancy rules are set by each province or territory and cover things such as notices, deposits, rent increases and eviction. Learn the rules for the exact place where you will rent before you buy, because they directly affect your income and your obligations.

The main risks

  • Leverage. Borrowing magnifies gains and losses.
  • Interest rates. Payments can rise at renewal.
  • Vacancy and non-payment.
  • Price changes. Values can fall as well as rise, and no return is guaranteed.
  • Illiquidity. Property cannot be sold quickly or cheaply.
  • Unrealistic promises. Be wary of anyone who guarantees returns, urges you to buy fast or asks you to sign before you have professional advice.

Housing costs and creative strategies

Some people look at ways to reduce or offset their own housing costs, such as renting out part of a home. These strategies depend on local rules, your lender's terms, your insurance and zoning, and they do not suit everyone. It is educational, the rules differ by country, and it does not guarantee any result.

A sensible way to learn

  1. Read official sources, including CMHC and the CRA.
  2. Speak to a mortgage professional, an accountant and a lawyer before you commit.
  3. Run conservative numbers that include vacancy, repairs and rate changes.
  4. Study your target area's rents, taxes and tenant rules.
  5. Start small, and never invest money you cannot afford to lose.

Frequently asked questions

Can newcomers buy property in Canada?

Often yes, but requirements differ by lender, status and province. CMHC's newcomer guide explains the process, and a mortgage professional can tell you what applies to you.

How much do I need as a down payment?

It depends on the price, the type of property and the lender, and the rules change. A smaller down payment generally requires mortgage loan insurance. Check CMHC and ask a lender about current requirements.

Is rental income taxable?

Generally yes. Owners report rental income and expenses on Form T776. Speak to an accountant about your situation.

Is real estate a guaranteed investment?

No. Values and rents can fall, costs can rise, and property is hard to sell quickly. No return is guaranteed.

Do I need a lawyer to buy a home?

In most cases a lawyer or notary is involved in closing a purchase, and rules differ by province. Ask a real estate professional where you plan to buy.

Where should I learn more?

Start with CMHC's homebuying resources and the CRA's rental income pages, then speak to a mortgage professional, an accountant and a lawyer.
