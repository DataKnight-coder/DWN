# How to Build Credit in Canada as a Newcomer (and Why It Matters)

Your credit history works a bit like a report card. Lenders, landlords and other businesses use it to decide whether to trust you. If you arrive in Canada with no credit history here, you start with a blank page, and a blank page can close doors you would never expect. This guide explains why credit matters, and the simple habits that build it, using Government of Canada sources.

## Why credit matters, in plain words

I teach this because so many people learn about credit only after it costs them an opportunity. Here is where it commonly shows up:

- **Renting a home.** Many landlords ask for a credit check before they rent to you. Thin or poor credit can cost you the place you wanted.
- **Some jobs.** Certain roles, such as positions of financial responsibility or work in banking, may involve a credit check as part of hiring.
- **Loans.** Personal loans, business loans, car loans and student loans all depend on how lenders see your credit.
- **A mortgage.** Lenders look closely at your credit history before they approve one.
- **A line of credit.** You generally need to have built credit first.
- **A phone plan.** Many carriers check your credit before they sign you to a plan.
- **Everyday services.** Some car rentals, hotels and parking services want a credit card, not a debit card.

Every country has its own system, and the rules differ in the United States, the United Kingdom and Australia. This guide is about Canada.

## Credit report and credit score: what they are

Your **credit report** is a record of your credit accounts and how you pay them. Your **credit score** is a number based on that record, commonly shown on a scale of about 300 to 900. Two main credit bureaus in Canada keep these records: Equifax and TransUnion. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) explains the basics in [Credit report and score basics](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/credit-report-score-basics.html).

Credit bureaus in Canada record your activity in Canada. A good history from another country usually does not appear on your Canadian credit report, so you build a new one here.

## Step 1: Check what is on your report

FCAC explains how to [get your credit report and score](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/order-credit-report.html), including free access through the two main bureaus. Check it for mistakes. If something is wrong, ask the bureau to correct it.

## Step 2: Start with a card designed for beginners

FCAC says a **secured credit card** can be an option if you have no credit history, and it names newcomers as an example. You put down a deposit, and the deposit usually sets your limit. Read the fees before you apply, as some cards charge a set-up fee or an annual fee. Choose a card from a recognized Canadian institution on a known network, as FCAC advises. See [choosing a credit card](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/choose-credit-card.html).

## Step 3: Pay on time, every time

FCAC calls payment history the most important part of your score. Late or missed payments hurt it. If you cannot pay the full balance, make at least the minimum payment, and read [paying off your credit card](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-cards/pay-off-credit-card.html).

## Step 4: Keep your balances low

FCAC points out that using most of your limit makes you look riskier. A common habit is to use only a small part of your limit and pay the full balance each month, which also saves interest.

## Step 5: Be patient and careful

- Keep older accounts open when you can, because a longer history helps.
- Do not apply for many cards or loans in a short time, because each application leaves a mark on your report.
- Over time, having more than one type of credit can help, but do not borrow just to build credit.
- Read FCAC's page on [improving your credit score](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/improve-credit-score.html).

## Watch out for credit repair scams

Be careful with anyone who promises to "fix" your credit for a fee, guarantees a score, or asks for your banking passwords. Legitimate help is free from the bureaus and from approved credit counselling agencies.

## What most newcomers get wrong

- Waiting until they need a loan or a rental to start.
- Using a debit card for everything, which does not build credit.
- Missing one payment and not realising how much it costs.
- Maxing out a new card.
- Believing credit works the same as in their home country.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to build credit in Canada?

It varies. A history builds over months of on-time payments, and a longer history helps more. Nobody can promise a timeline or a score.

### Do I have a credit score when I arrive?

Usually not a Canadian one. Bureaus record Canadian activity, so you start by opening accounts and using them well.

### Does a debit card build credit?

Generally no. Credit is built by borrowing and repaying, such as with a credit card or a loan.

### Is a secured credit card worth it?

FCAC says it can be an option for people with no credit history, including newcomers. Compare fees and choose a recognized issuer.

### Can I check my own credit without hurting it?

Yes. Checking your own report is different from a lender checking it when you apply, and FCAC explains how to get your report.

### What is a good credit score in Canada?

Bureaus describe ranges slightly differently. Check the bureau's own explanation of its ranges rather than relying on a single number.
