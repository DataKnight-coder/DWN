# How to Become a UGC Creator: Portfolio, Pitching and Contracts

UGC stands for user-generated content. A UGC creator makes content such as short videos, photos and reviews for a brand, in the style of an everyday customer, and the brand uses it on its own channels and in its advertising. You are paid for the content, and you often do not need a large following, because the brand publishes it rather than you. This guide explains how people get started, how pitching and contracts work, and what to protect yourself against. There is no guaranteed income, and rates vary widely.

## What UGC creators actually do

- **Create content for a brand** following a brief: product demos, unboxings, reviews, tutorials and "day in the life" clips.
- **Deliver files** that the brand posts or runs as ads, instead of posting on your own account.
- **Sometimes post as well,** which is a different job with different disclosure duties; see below.

## Getting started

1. **Pick a niche you can speak about:** beauty, home, food, fitness, tech, parenting, pets or services. Authentic knowledge shows on camera.
2. **Create a handful of strong samples** in two or three formats, even using products you already own. Sample quality matters more than quantity.
3. **Build a simple portfolio** on a free site, a document or a profile that anyone can open with one link.
4. **Practise the basics:** natural light, clear sound, a steady phone, a short hook in the first seconds, and honest, specific language.
5. **Learn the brief.** Brands want content that matches their message, so follow the instructions and ask questions before you film.

## Finding work

Creators usually need to start the conversation. Common routes are:

- **Direct pitches:** a short email to brands whose products fit your style, saying what you make, who it suits and linking examples. Avoid generic messages sent to everyone.
- **Platforms and marketplaces** that connect creators and brands. Read their terms, fees and payment rules carefully, and never pay to be listed.
- **Your own network:** small businesses you already know often need content and have no time to make it.

## Contracts and usage rights

This is where beginners lose money. Settle these in writing before you film:

- **Scope:** how many videos or photos, formats, length and revisions.
- **Payment:** the amount, the due date and what happens if the brand cancels.
- **Usage rights:** where, how long and in what form the brand may use your content, such as organic social posts, paid ads, a website or email. Usage that is broader or longer is normally priced higher, so agree it explicitly.
- **Exclusivity:** whether you may work with competing brands during or after the job.
- **Portfolio rights:** make sure you are allowed to show the work in your own portfolio.
- **Ownership:** who owns the final content and the raw files.

A handshake or an email thread is a weak foundation. Even a signed one-page agreement covering scope, pay, payment date and usage is better than nothing. If a deal involves significant money or broad rights, consider having a lawyer review it.

## Disclosure and honesty

If you post content and you have a relationship with a brand, such as payment or free products, you must disclose it clearly. The Competition Bureau explains that [material connections should be disclosed clearly and prominently](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/deceptive-marketing-practices/types-deceptive-marketing-practices/influencer-marketing-and-competition-act). Never claim to use a product you do not use, and never repeat a brand claim you cannot support.

## Money, tax and status

Keep a record of every payment and expense. In Canada, income from content work is generally reportable; see the CRA's [guidance for people who earn through digital platforms](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/programs/about-canada-revenue-agency-cra/compliance/platform-economy/understanding-tax-obligations.html). If you hold a work permit, check that self-employment is allowed under your conditions; [IRCC explains that permits may restrict the type of work](https://www.ircc.canada.ca/english/helpcentre/answer.asp?qnum=169&top=17). Rates vary enormously by creator, niche, format and usage, so do not assume any number you see online.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting before you have samples.** Brands ask to see work first.
- **Accepting unlimited usage for a low fee.** Always define where and for how long.
- **No written terms.** Disputes over payment and usage are common without them.
- **Paying to get "hired".** A genuine brand or platform does not charge you for a job.
- **Ignoring tax records.** Track income from the first payment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a large following to be a UGC creator?

No. Brands usually publish the content themselves, so they care more about the quality and fit of your content than your follower count.

### Do I need professional equipment?

Not to begin. A modern phone, good natural light and clear sound are enough for many briefs.

### How much do UGC creators earn?

It varies widely by niche, format, brand and usage rights, and no income is guaranteed. Price your work using scope and usage, and track what each job really costs you in time.

### What are usage rights?

They define where, how long and in what form a brand can use your content, such as paid ads or its website. Broader or longer usage is normally priced higher and should be written down.

### Do I have to disclose a brand relationship?

If you post content and have a material connection with a brand, yes, clearly and prominently. See the Competition Bureau's guidance.

### Can I do this on a work permit?

It depends on your permit conditions. Check with IRCC guidance or a qualified adviser before you take paid work.
